Your doctor may have ordered a blood test for you as part of a regular health routine or in anticipation of a surgery or other health procedure. But what exactly are doctors looking for when they order a blood test?

A blood test involves using a syringe needle to collect a sample of blood, which is then sent to a lab and tested. Some tests, referred to as panel tests, measure a wide range of blood contents. The most common of these is the complete blood count (CBC), which measures the volume of red and white blood cells, hemoglobin, and platelets in the blood.

Other blood tests are designed to detect something specific, such as a hormone, marker, enzyme, or protein that may indicate you have a particular condition.

Understanding the Importance of Blood Tests

While blood tests alone are not sufficient to determine whether or not you have a medical condition, they are a powerful diagnostic tool for doctors as they evaluate your health.

There are a number of reasons a doctor might order a blood test:

As part of your regular preventative health routine to evaluate your overall health

In anticipation of a surgery or treatment to make sure the treatment plan is appropriate and safe A screening test for any conditions you may be at risk of developing, i.e. if you have a family history of cancer or heart disease

To determine the cause of symptoms you may be experiencing

Your doctor may order regular testing throughout a treatment for specific medical conditions to monitor whether or not the regimen is working effectively

Blood tests empower doctors in a number of ways. First, they can highlight potential health risks before any external symptoms become noticeable, allowing your doctor to help you make the changes necessary to avoid developing a health condition. For health conditions that may have already developed, blood tests can help narrow down potential causes of symptoms, allowing doctors to address the root cause of the issue and hopefully prevent further complications from developing. Finally, tests allow doctors to monitor your health beyond noticeable symptoms, allowing them to tailor treatment to your health needs.

Basics of Blood Test Results and Their Meaning

It’s important to note that you should never try to interpret your blood test results without the guidance of a licensed and trained healthcare professional, as factors like age, health conditions, medications, and more can influence results. Because blood tests are designed to detect and measure blood content differently, there are a few ways to consider the results.

Blood test results can be formatted differently depending on the lab providing them, but are required by law to include mandatory information such as the name of the physician who ordered the test, the name of the healthcare professionals who did the test, and the date the test was completed.

There are a few different ways results may be indicated on the report:

Positive/Negative: This type of test result is usually associated with a particular condition. An example of this would be a blood test to determine whether or not a person is pregnant.

Quantitative Result: These test results show the measured quantity of what the test was designed to detect.

Reference Range: Each lab generally has its own range for each item being measured in a test. These are usually indicated next to your quantitative result so that you can see where your measurement falls within the range.

To get an idea of what a doctor may infer from a blood test, here is a breakdown of a CBC panel and what the results may indicate.

Hematocrit levels: Hematocrit refers to the volume of red blood cells in your blood. A high hematocrit result can indicate dehydration, while low hematocrit may indicate anemia.

Hemoglobin levels: Hemoglobin, an iron-rich protein found in blood cells, helps carry oxygen throughout the body. Low hemoglobin is associated with anemia, thalassemia, and sickle cell disease.

Mean Corpuscular Volume (MCV): MCV is the measurement of your average red blood cell size. Low MCV can indicate anemia or thalassemia.

Platelet Levels: Platelets are composed of blood cell fragments that aid clotting by sticking together at the site of breaks in blood vessels to stop bleeding. Platelet results outside of the normal range may indicate a bleeding or clotting disorder.

Red Blood Cell Count: Red blood cells carry oxygen from the lungs to the rest of the body. Red blood cell volume outside a normal range may indicate bleeding, anemia, or dehydration.

White Blood Cell Count: White blood cells play an essential role in the immune system to fight illness and disease. White blood cell levels outside a normal range can signal infection, an immune disorder, or cancer.

It’s important to note that results being on the higher or lower end of the normal range may be due to other health factors and isn’t necessarily a sign that something is wrong. Your doctor may order the test to be done again to confirm the results and make sure they aren’t the result of bad blood work, an issue resulting from improper testing or handling. If your doctor feels your test results may indicate a health issue, the next steps to address the problem might include ordering more tests, prescribing medication, and/or suggesting lifestyle changes.

Common Blood Conditions

Two of the most common blood conditions identified by blood tests are anemia and high cholesterol. In this section, we review what these conditions are and how you can address them.

Anemia: Understanding and Management

Managing High Cholesterol

Approximately one-fourth of the world’s population is affected by anemia. A diagnosis of anemia means your body doesn’t have enough red blood cells, which are responsible for carrying oxygen to other organs.

This lack of circulated oxygen can lead to symptoms such as:

Chest pain and shortness of breath

Dizziness and pulsatile tinnitus (pounding or whooshing sounds in ears)

Irregular or rapid heartbeat and headache

There are many causes of anemia, such as genetic causes, injury or illness, and nutritional deficiency.

The two types of anemia related to nutritional deficiency most frequently seen include:

Iron-Deficiency Anemia: This is the most prevalent type of anemia, accounting for roughly 50% of anemia cases. It can be caused by blood loss, not consuming enough iron, or health conditions that negatively affect iron absorption. If not addressed, iron-deficiency anemia can cause severe health issues.

Pernicious anemia: This type of anemia occurs due to low levels of vitamin b12, also known as folate. Typically, it is the result of not getting enough vitamin B12 in your diet. It can also result from a lack of intrinsic factor, a protein in the stomach that aids vitamin B12 absorption. Additional symptoms of this type of anemia include soreness of mouth and tongue, numbness and tingling in limbs, and cognitive effects like confusion, memory issues, and depression.

Treating these types of anemia often includes switching to a more nutritious diet and treating any underlying causes of deficiency. Your doctor may also recommend supplements to supply your body with the nutrients it needs. Supplements may be taken orally, given as a shot, or administered via IV therapy.

If your blood work readings showed that you have high cholesterol, also known as hyperlipidemia or hypercholesterol, this means that there are too many lipids, or fats, in your blood. While lipids are necessary for healthy body function, having more than your body can use will result in buildup in your arteries as they combine with other blood components to form fatty deposits known as plaque. Over time, this can become dangerous, causing cardiovascular disease.

Blood testing is incredibly important to detect high cholesterol, as there typically aren’t obvious signs until damage has already been done. Hypercholesterol often leads to hypertension, or high blood pressure, which can cause symptoms such as fatigue, impaired vision, headaches, trouble breathing, chest pains, and irregular heartbeat. In extremely severe cases, high cholesterol can cause xanthoma, a skin condition resulting in waxy deposits under the skin. These deposits are typically found around the joints, eyes, and hands.

If you are wondering how to improve blood test results after a diagnosis of high cholesterol, it will inevitably involve lifestyle changes that may include exercise, quitting smoking, and changing to a healthier diet. Depending on how high your cholesterol levels are, your doctor may also prescribe medications or refer you to a specialist.

Some examples of dietary changes to lower cholesterol include:

Consuming less foods high in saturated fats, cholesterol, and trans fats. Examples include red meats, high-fat dairy products, and egg yolks

Incorporating more fiber into your diet, like vegetables, whole grains, and fruits

Avoiding fried foods, as well as highly processed foods like fast foods or prepackaged foods made with palm oil or cocoa butter

Eating lean proteins like legumes, chicken, and fish

Medications to treat cholesterol can work to lower cholesterol production in different ways.

Statins like Lipitor work by blocking the production of cholesterol in the liver.

Zetia is an example of a cholesterol absorption inhibitor

Vytorin contains a combination of active ingredients that both block cholesterol production and limit cholesterol absorption in the intestine.

Gender-Specific Concerns

Age-Specific Blood Health

Statistics on health conditions vary according to gender, meaning that doctors often consider gender when evaluating your health.

Women are especially at risk of cardiovascular disease. Compared to men, they are at higher risk of stroke and are more likely to die after a heart attack. They are also more likely to develop urinary tract issues and osteoarthritis. It should also be noted that excess, chronic alcohol consumption has a greater impact on women, putting them at higher risk of heart disease and breast cancer.

Men over 50 have a higher risk of developing skin cancer, prostate cancer, and colorectal cancer. They are also at risk of cardiovascular diseases, which are especially heightened by unhealthy lifestyle choices that are more common in men.

People receiving gender-affirming hormone therapy are at a higher risk of breast cancer and developing bone density issues regardless of which hormones they are taking. Those receiving feminizing hormone therapy are at risk of prostate cancer and should receive age-appropriate screenings recommended for men. Those receiving masculinizing hormone therapy are at higher risk of cervical dysplasia resulting from the thinning of cervical tissues. They should also receive age-appropriate screenings for cervical cancer recommended for women.

Genetics in Blood Health

Emerging Research

There are countless health conditions that can run in a family, which can be caused by both genetic and shared environmental factors. Your doctor is likely to look for signs of any conditions from your family health history in your blood test results.

Type 2 diabetes is one example of a hereditary condition in which blood test results can be critical. Regular testing can reveal when people who are genetically predisposed to diabetes have developed prediabetes, a condition involving higher than normal blood sugar levels that may develop into Type 2 diabetes if left untreated. If prediabetes is diagnosed, your doctor can work with you to make a plan to bring blood sugar levels down and avoid type 2 diabetes. This plan may include lifestyle modifications and, if necessary, medication.

Heart disease is another condition in which genetics play a factor. Cholesterol and blood sugar levels both play a role in the development of heart disease. Knowing you are at higher risk of developing heart disease can help your doctor monitor your blood test results more closely and develop a plan if your results indicate heart disease could potentially develop. As with diabetes, your doctor can work with you to make a plan to reduce the risk of developing heart disease, including medications and lifestyle changes.

Researchers are constantly working to determine how to detect health conditions earlier to increase the chances of avoiding more serious health complications. People unable to access family health history may want to consider genetic blood tests, which can help determine what conditions you are predisposed to developing. People usually hear about these tests in the context of determining what conditions may be passed onto children, but these tests can offer insight into your own health concerns as well.

Practical Tips

Although blood tests are routine, preparing for them properly can make a big difference in the outcome of your results. The type of preparation you will need to do may vary depending on what is being tested. Here, we outline the general tips on how to prepare for your blood test. If you want to know how to improve your bloodwork, check in with your doctor before the test to ensure you don’t do anything that could affect the results.

Preparing for Blood Tests - Before the Test

Getting ready for a blood test generally starts the day before your test. Fasting, meaning you don’t consume any food or drink except water, is a common requirement for a number of diagnostics. The timeframe of fasting is generally 8 – 12 hours before the test. People often try to schedule these tests in the morning so that they can sleep their way through most of the fast. Some blood tests may also require that you not consume alcohol for a certain period of time before the test. It should be noted that during a fast, you should also avoid chewing gum, exercising, and smoking.

On the day of the test, make sure you are hydrated, as more fluid in your veins will make the process easier. However, caffeinated drinks such as coffee, tea, and energy drinks should be avoided. If you are worried you might get nauseous, don’t eat right before your appointment – even if you aren’t required to fast. Those without a fasting requirement should eat 1-2 hours prior to the test to avoid lightheadedness.

There are several other factors to consider to be prepared for your blood test.

Make sure to wear short sleeves so that the nurse can access your arm.

You should also make sure to have your identification and insurance card with you.

You may need to fill out some paperwork before the blood draw is performed, so be sure to arrive early to complete any paperwork so your blood draw can be performed on time.

Finally, you may want to bring a snack with you to eat after your appointment, especially if your test requires you to fast.

Preparing for Blood Tests - During the Test

Blood draws are generally quick, straightforward procedures that are finished in minutes. They typically take place in labs, in rooms designed for the procedure.

Here’s what to expect during the procedure:

You will be asked to sit in a chair with an armrest with your arm outstretched.

The site of the blood draw – often the inner elbow – will be sanitized with a small wipe, and a stretchy band will be tied around your arm.

You may also be asked to make a fist to promote blood flow.

At this point, the needle will be inserted, and the blood draw will begin.
