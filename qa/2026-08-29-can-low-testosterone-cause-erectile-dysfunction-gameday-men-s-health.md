Erectile dysfunction is one of the most common sexual health concerns men experience, affecting between 30 and 50 million men in the United States alone. While low testosterone can play a role in overall sexual health, the connection between testosterone levels and erectile dysfunction is more complex. However, erectile function depends on multiple systems working together, including blood vessels, nerves, hormones, and psychological health.

Understanding whether your erectile dysfunction is being caused by low testosterone or a different factor is critical. In this guide, we’ll examine the relationship between testosterone and erectile function, what signs to look out for, and what other things may be causing ED.

What Role Does Testosterone Play in Sexual Health?

Testosterone plays a key role in the overall state of men's health, but its influence on erections is often misunderstood. The primary sexual function influenced by testosterone is libido, or sexual desire. When testosterone levels are healthy, most men experience normal interest in sex, arousal, and sexual-based motivation.

Testosterone levels also support energy levels, confidence, and overall mood. These factors may not seem directly related to erections, but they can significantly influence sexual performance. A man who feels fatigued, irritable, or disengaged is less likely to feel sexually responsive or confident, even if blood flow is technically adequate to achieve and maintain an erection.Erections themselves rely primarily on vascular and neurological function. Sexual stimulation triggers the release of nitric oxide, which relaxes blood vessels in the penis and allows increased blood flow. This process depends far more on cardiovascular health and nerve signaling than on testosterone levels alone. (2)

Can Low Testosterone Actually Cause Erectile Dysfunction?

Low testosterone can contribute to erectile dysfunction, but it is rarely the sole cause. In men with clinically low testosterone (typically considered to be lower than 300 ng/dL) erections may become weaker, less frequent, or less reliable over time. (3) This is more likely when low testosterone has been present for an extended period and is accompanied by other symptoms.

However, most cases of erectile dysfunction are driven primarily by blood flow issues. Conditions that affect the cardiovascular system, such as high blood pressure, atherosclerosis, and diabetes, are far more likely to impair erections than hormone levels alone. (4) This is why erectile dysfunction is often considered an early warning sign of cardiovascular disease, as these conditions can cause a reduction in blood flow, leading to ED.

Signs ED May Be Linked to Low Testosterone

Certain patterns can suggest that low testosterone may be contributing to erectile dysfunction, especially when erectile issues are not occurring in isolation. These may include: (5)

Reduced libido or minimal interest in sex

Persistent fatigue that does not improve with rest

Mood changes such as low motivation, irritability, or mild depression

Loss of muscle mass or increased body fat despite similar activity levels

Other Common Causes of Erectile Dysfunction

Erectile dysfunction often has multiple contributing factors. Even when low testosterone is present, other issues are usually involved.

Cardiovascular health is one of the most important factors. Erections require healthy blood vessels that can dilate properly. Conditions such as high cholesterol, high blood pressure, and heart disease can reduce blood flow and impair erectile quality. Diabetes is another major contributor. Elevated blood sugar levels can damage blood vessels and nerves over time, making erections more difficult and less predictable. (6)

Psychological factors can also play a significant role in causing problems with erections. Stress, anxiety, and performance pressure can interfere with arousal and make erectile dysfunction worse. Even when a physical cause is the driving force of ED, mental stress and anxiety can amplify the problem.

Certain medications, including antidepressants, blood pressure medications, and prostate treatments, may also affect erectile function as a side effect. (7) If you’re concerned that a medication you’re taking is causing ED, talk to your doctor about potential alternatives.

Lifestyle factors such as poor sleep, excess alcohol consumption, smoking, physical inactivity, and obesity can also contribute to erectile dysfunction by affecting hormones, circulation, and overall energy levels. (8)

Testosterone levels are evaluated using blood tests, typically taken in the morning when levels are thought to be highest. Total testosterone is usually the first marker measured, as it provides a broad view of hormone status.

In some cases, additional testing may be needed. Free testosterone, sex hormone binding globulin, and other related markers can provide more insight on your overall health profile, particularly when symptoms are present but total testosterone levels appear borderline.

Symptoms alone are not enough to diagnose low testosterone. For instance, ED symptoms like fatigue, reduced libido, and mood changes can also result from stress, sleep deprivation, poor nutrition, or other underlying medical conditions. Blood testing provides objective data that helps determine whether testosterone levels are truly below a healthy range.

Testosterone exists on a spectrum, and your physician will consider factors like age, specific symptoms, your health history, and your overall goals when making a treatment determination.

Treating ED When Low Testosterone Is a Factor

When low testosterone is identified as a contributing factor through testing, treatment often involves a layered approach instead of one standardized solution.

Hormone optimization may help improve libido, energy, mood, and overall sexual desire. In men with a confirmed testosterone deficiency, restoring levels to a healthy range can help improve quality of life and confidence levels in men. However, hormone therapy alone may not fully resolve erectile dysfunction if other causes are present.

ED-specific treatments focus directly on improving erectile function. These therapies are designed to enhance blood flow and support the physical mechanics of an erection. They can be effective regardless of testosterone levels and are often used alongside hormone treatment when appropriate.
