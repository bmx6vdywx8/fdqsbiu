# How This Secret Body Armor Is Keeping an Anonymous, Injured Billionaire Safe

You’ve got an extremely high net worth. The world is yours for the buying. Want a €100 million yacht, with Italian marble floors, moored in the South of France? Sure. A smaller €30 million yacht to ferry you to the big one? Why not? A fleet of seven-figure supercars, all “one of a very limited number”? Absolutely. Fridays are for deciding which of your sprawling mansions in Monaco, Zurich, or Dubai to aim the Gulfstream towards for the weekend.

However, there is one thing that’s almost impossible to purchase, despite an overflowing bank account: peace of mind about your health.

Meet Client X. He’s a mega billionaire with a secret. A series of intensive operations have left him with a compromised rib cage. Specifically, his fourth and fifth ribs have markedly impaired functionality, as does his sternum. Those deficiencies leave the vital organs beneath at serious risk of grave harm. Even the little things hurt; an errant elbow from a crowded cocktail party, a snug seat belt, toting a heavy backpack, prolonged sitting in a stiff chair. Outwardly, you’d never know his plight. The uber-wealthy never wish to appear weak, either publicly or amongst elite peers, so Client X kept his issues shrouded, every wince turned into a smile and he went about his day.

With an increased sense of frailty and a mounting concern for his weakened thorax, Client X severely limited his exuberant lifestyle. There were fewer outings in supercars on race circuits, more unattended black tie galas, fewer lift tickets to the slopes at Zermatt. These may sound like pretentious rich man problems, but when you’ve worked hard for your fortune, you’d like to enjoy the fruits of your labor, not greet each day in fear.

With few viable medical avenues left to pursue, Client X turned to McLaren Applied Technologies. You’re most likely familiar with McLaren as a provider of exemplary supercars—such as the 570S and the 720S—to the world’s most discerning and affluent gearheads (one of whom is indeed Client X). Or perhaps the motorsport group, responsible for fielding McLaren’s championship Formula One team. Despite being synonymous with fast automobiles, the quickest growing sector of the British company is actually its health and wellness sector. Two principal goals anchor the consumer portion of this division: mobility improvement and protection of the human body. Client X’s dilemma presented a unique challenge on both fronts, and it immediately became a hero project of the group.

“Client X came to us wanting to feel less fragile,” Dan Toon, principal of McLaren’s design team and the project lead, told Men’s Health. “He wanted to feel normal.” The simplicity of that initial ask belied the plethora of complexities and situations the final product would have to suit. A staggering amount of variables would have to be calculated when concepting a solution.

“Part of our team mantra is to explore every possible path and give any issue 150 percent of our efforts. This was no different,” said Toon. “What we ultimately enabled Client X to do is feel invincible.”

Implicit with being a premium brand is the expectation of high-end personal specifications, but McLaren treated Project Invincible as a bespoke endeavor of the highest order. (And a super secret one, at that. Only seven people within McLaren know the name of Client X—none of whom were willing to share said information with Men’s Health.)

“First, we had to understand the day in the life of a billionaire, which is quite different than yours and mine,” Toon laughed. “What are his daily activities? What do they require in terms of demands on his body?” For Client X, driving the fastest cars in existence, skiing, hiking and being in the middle of a throng of people were among the prime considerations.

“Next, we needed to know everything about his health, so we got his full medical history and gathered our own data,” Toon said. Medical information was gleaned over several meetings earlier in 2017, “at various places around the world.” While declining to reveal what the initial catastrophic event was that led to the resulting surgeries, Toon disclosed that Client X “had a major surgery several years ago and another one in the last year,” noting his restricted activity regimen had been ongoing for the last 18 months.

“This man had undergone a lot of surgery. He looked fragile,” said Toon. “He’d been through a lot of trauma and still had some ongoing issues. He had a constant fear about new injuries and making his condition worse.” While in the room with Client X, Toon and his associates conducted CT scans and 3D body scans to understand what was going on inside as well as the geometry of the billionaire’s body.

Armed with a heap of empirical and anecdotal data, the crew at McLaren Applied Technologies’ Woking headquarters established a set of overarching requirements for Project Invincible. “We had to protect the vital organs of the body and augment the function of the rib cage but do it in such a way that the protected rib cage and structures within were contained,” Toon said.

Simultaneously, the constraints for the device were assessed. “This item needed to be discreet enough to be worn under a dress shirt without being noticed, and the shape needed to be individualized to the client so the fit was impeccably close, all while being able to attenuate a serious impact or load,” said Broderick Coburn, a composites design engineer for McLaren.

“Client X came to us wanting to feel less fragile. What we enabled him to do is feel invincible.”

Mechanical engineers, composite engineers, designers and other members of the team from a variety of expertises came together and began broadly thinking about the challenge. Various ideas and design mockups were sketched on Post-It notes or on a white board. “That initial brainstorm session was purposefully loose for two reasons. One, anything could be the final design, and two, anything can take you in a direction you’d never thought you’d go,” Coburn said.

Some initial ideas looked like an exo-skeleton rib cage, others more like nesting plates of body armor, and then someone tossed out the idea of a shield. “That design resonated most because we knew it could transfer the load of an impact quite a distance,” Coburn said. For example, traditional body armor fits flush against the body and dissipates a blow or impact locally, relying on your rib cage to diffuse the bulk of the force. It lacks the structural stiffness to sufficiently transfer the energy from a load to where McLaren would want. “We needed a hit to chest to send the load into Client X’s lower, functioning ribs on the left and right and then up to the manubrium, at the top of the sternum,” Toon said. “A rigid shield could accomplish that.”

A shield on its own would not be enough, however, so the full protection system would consist of three components: a custom base layer garment, the composite shield and impact attenuation pads which would interface between and adhere the shield and base garment. With the 3D scan of Client X’s torso, a mannequin had been built, perfectly to scale. Various shield and base garment design options were 3D printed and affixed to the mannequin, to determine sizing and fit. About a month later, the concept had moved from rudimentary sketch into the prototype phase.

“We thought about the solution in totality, in terms of the overall needs of the system. We wanted a base layer garment that the shield could affix to because Client X should be able to remove it when not necessary, but also so that he could be encouraged to wear this all the time,” Toon said. “He should put his underwear on, then his base layer garment and, at any point, he can add the shield.”

If maximum efficacy means wearing constantly, comfortable yet durable materials become paramount. “The base layer is a construction of natural and artificial fibers, designed so as not to hinder breathing but be extremely form-fitting, possess wicking characteristics, and have the structural integrity to be able to hold the shield in place. You don’t want a loose garment here,” Coburn said.

While the fibers used are not unique—cotton polyester elastane for absorption and tencel polyester elastane for wicking—the method and process of entwining the two into one fabric was proprietary to this project, Coburn shared. Once the fabric was constructed, it was tailored to Client X via the body scan data in conjunction with traditional tailor measurements. Included is a Velcro tensioning mechanism which helps snug everything up and ready the base garment for the shield’s attachment.

There’s also one other hidden benefit to the base layer: kidney protection. “Client X’s kidneys are slightly compromised, so we wanted an additional layer support for that region,” Toon explained. “We inserted a soft, non-Newtonian silicon foam pad around that area.”

As for the shield materials, carbon fiber alone wouldn’t solve the problem. “It met our criteria of being lightweight and stiff enough, but it fails in a very brittle manner, like a plastic ruler splintering when it snaps. You don’t want a mass of splinters exploding near your chest,” Coburn said. What was needed was more ductility so it could fail gradually, like a metal ruler bending but never fully fracturing. Layering a number of other fibers alongside carbon was the ticket.

Zylon, the first organic fiber whose cross-sectional strength outperforms both steel and carbon fiber, was added. (Zylon is now used in all Formula One cars after a request from the governing body for teams to increase protection for drivers. The first team to utilize this strongest man-made material? McLaren-Honda.) The Zylon, used for penetration resistance and enhanced energy absorption during an impact, is hidden in the core, since it’s sensitive to ultraviolet light. Also included were high-failure straint Dyneema fibers, most commonly used in bulletproof vests for damage containment, but please don’t shoot the billionaire. “Ballistic grade wasn’t a shield requirement and we didn’t case test against bullets,” explained Coburn.

To form the actual shield, the raw materials are cut in the proper shapes, laid into a mold, then a resin is poured in. Into an autoclave the shield goes, where high temperatures and pressure are applied to compact all three fibers together and cure the resin. That resin is new, from McLaren’s Formula One supplier, trying to get them to switch over. Toon claims it’s tougher than traditional resin and won’t fracture as easily. After the autoclave comes a fine polishing–the baking and polishing were done in conjunction with McLaren’s F1 team, at the hands of skilled F1 laborers–and within 24 hours, you’ve got one invincible shield for a secret billionaire.

The most important material is between the shield and the base garment. “Like with the kidney buffer, we used a non-Newtonian, viscoelastic silicone gel,” said Chief Medical Officer Dr. Adam Hill. “It’s similar to a silly putty, and it’s under each of the points where the shield meets the body. The auxetic geometrical makeup means it can diffuse just about any load the shield would be exposed to.”

With the prototype complete, testing commenced. There was user testing, with Client X gauging how well the garment fit and felt on a daily basis. Trivial issues with movement of the shield when getting in or out of a supercar or going from sitting to standing were easily corrected with tweaks to the tensioning straps.

The static testing for strength was more interesting. “For static case testing, we put dead weight onto the shield and saw how much it moved,” said James Helliwell, the mechanical engineer in charge of testing. About 150 kilograms was placed onto the shield, which weighs a mere 250 grams. It deflected less than 5 millimeters. That’s equivalent to a 330-pound sumo wrestler standing on top of a hamster and the hamster being unscathed.

Impact testing was where things got downright fascinating. Carried out at the Cranfield Impact Center – where the homologation crash tests for Formula One monocoques are performed – the tests consist of dropping differing masses from varying heights directly onto the shield. First, there was a 30 Joule impact, which is akin to dropping a 15-pound bowling ball onto your chest from waist height. “That’s a pretty substantial impact event,” Helliwell explained, “and the shield passed easily.”

After repeating the 30 Joule test multiple times, and being unable to reach the shield’s failure threshold, the engineers moved onto other impact case scenarios – from the recoil of a champagne bottle hitting your chest to a seat belt snapping taut under hard deceleration to a professional karate punch – and the shield passed each. “So we wanted to try a more extreme example,” said Helliwell.

That would be a 150 Joule impact event, effectively dropping 5 bowling balls, or 75 pounds, onto your chest from a few feet up. “The desired result was that the shield retain its basic shape, in one piece, but crack. It’s like a crumple zone on a car during a crash; it does its job but it will need to be replaced. That’s precisely what we observed with the 150 Joule test.”

Three months after the beleaguered billionaire that is Client X asked McLaren Applied Technologies for help, they had a final product to present to him. Toon declined to give the exact price they charged for Project Invincible, though said it was under $250,000. When you’re talking about that amount of money and something so special, you don’t just want to unceremoniously hand the product over.

“We manufactured this beautiful presentation box to place it in, somewhere to keep the shield when not in use,” Toon said. “A presentation booklet was made, too, chronicling the story of the build. Two months ago, we invited Client X into our Technology Center here in Woking and showed him the video we shot [above], then presented him the box with the shield. He was very impressed.”

That beautiful box from McLaren was far more than an innovative piece of body armor; it represented the return of Client X’s life. Given that, you’d assume the man bestowed effusive gratitude and platitudes upon the intimate team who’d worked doggedly to accomplish this incredible feat. You’d be wrong. “He’s a billionaire so you don’t get too much praise from him,” Toon remarked, “but he kept doing this nodding of approval throughout our film, so that felt good.”

Ultimately, Client X’s appreciation was conveyed, albeit via an unorthodox manner. “We had to sign all these non-disclosure agreements before we started, because Client X wanted the utmost discretion and secrecy,” said Toon. “Since we’ve delivered it to him, he’s been far from discrete with it. He’s so proud to show it off that he told his mates immediately.”

“We’re now getting a ton of interest from high net-worth individuals who say Client X recommended us,” Dr. Hill said. “They have similar issues and want similar solutions. For someone in that position to go out as our advocate is a huge deal, since he has no need to.” Dr. Hill added that McLaren’s Applied Technologies solutions aren’t just for the wealthy; the company aims and strives to better the lives of every person who could use assistance.

Client X has reported, through several of his personal assistants, that he’s over the moon for the device and continues to use it daily for a number of implications, and hasn’t had any noticeable impacts. “He’ll be stepping up in the coming months to get back to motor racing again,” Dr. Hill noted. Here’s hoping he feels invincible out on the track.

WHAT IS MCLAREN HUMAN PERFORMANCE?

Overseen by Chief Medical Officer Dr. Adam Hill, McLaren Human Performance is a division of McLaren Applied Technologies that dovetails nicely into the group’s growing healthcare sector. Dr. Hill, the touchpoint between McLaren and Client X, explains that McLaren’s human performance focus has grown over the last ten years to help optimize performances of race car drivers throughout their career.

“Whether they’re at a very young age all the way up to a seated Formula One driver, we’re interested in helping them achieve their maximum potential, as well as optimizing the performances from our pit crew, who are equally integral to the success of the car. One-tenth of a second can be the difference between first and tenth place over the course of a 100-minute Formula One race,” Dr. Hill said.

“Changing tires in a short a time as humanly possible is vital and that’s a physical activity. It’s not an individual activity, but a team sport that requires trust between the driver and the crew. That requires coaching to develop physical and psychological attributes for the whole team, so maintaining a level of fitness and readiness to perform is always a challenge. McLaren Human Performance doctors and the lab in Woking has expertise in supporting that effort.

“In building this institutional knowledge over the past decade, there are many ways to use this information we’re collecting. We use this to deliver data-driven design. By capturing data on humans under stresses and experimental conditions, we can alter the design of a protective device or a piece of sports equipment or even data collection devices as wearables. We have experience with developing all of this in our labs.

“We do get approached from time to time to engage in hero projects, such as with Client X and Project Invincible. We have a history of this which stretches back over the last decade or so, but this is the most personalized hero project McLaren Applied Technologies has completed, and the one most aligned with health care. Down the line, we’ve got plenty more projects coming and we’re developing implantable and wearable medical devices, though the mainstay of the consumer business will always be augmenting or improving mobility and the protection of humans, as we did for Client X. Within the bounds of the possible, we will always try to deliver and delight our customers.”
