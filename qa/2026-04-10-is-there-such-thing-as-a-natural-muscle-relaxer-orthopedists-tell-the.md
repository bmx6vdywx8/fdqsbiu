# Is There Such Thing as a Natural Muscle Relaxer? Orthopedists Tell the Truth.

IF YOU OFTEN have achy biceps after an intense workout or a sore back when you sit for too long, there are plenty of medications that can help. But if you’re looking for a natural muscle relaxer, you may have wondered about the supplements that claim to relieve sore muscles.

“Some supplements can support muscle recovery, but none function as true muscle relaxers the way prescription medications do,” says Kelechi Okoroha, M.D., an orthopedist and chief of sports medicine at the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center in Dallas.

Can supplements work as natural muscle relaxers?

“SUPPLEMENTS CANNOT RELAX skeletal muscle, but may be useful for muscle soreness,” says Michael Swartzon, M.D., a sports medicine physician with Baptist Health Orthopedic Care, part of Baptist Health South Florida.

For instance, Dr. Okoroha says certain supplements can reduce inflammation, support muscle repair, and improve sleep, which may relieve soreness. Here are some examples:

Tart cherry juice

There’s mixed evidence that antioxidants like tart cherry juice (anthocyanins) may reduce soreness and inflammation, Dr. Swartzon says, adding that some professional athletes use it to minimize delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS). It doesn’t actually relax muscle tissue, though.

Chamomile

Chamomile can help relax smooth muscle but not skeletal muscle: “Think GI system, not biceps muscles,” Dr. Swartzon says. It’s traditionally used for cramps and relaxation, which can help with sleep and indirectly support recovery, says Taiyyibah Moughal, R.D.N., a sports dietitian and founder of Empowered Beast Nutrition.

Turmeric

Turmeric has low-grade anti-inflammatory properties, Dr. Swartzon says. “It may reduce the inflammatory cascade that leads to muscle soreness by reducing exercise-induced muscle damage.” Research suggests that turmeric may also reduce DOMS after exercise, lower inflammation biomarkers, and boost antioxidants.

Cayenne

When used topically, cayenne, or capsaicin, can increase blood flow and minimize pain signals, Dr. Okoroha says. So you might feel less pain, but it doesn’t actually relax muscles, Dr. Swartzon says.

Magnesium

It’s often recommended for muscle cramps, especially if you’re magnesium-deficient, Dr. Swartzon says. But he adds that it may also improve muscle soreness and sleep. However, a “food-first approach,” rather than supplements, is typically best for getting enough magnesium (and other nutrients), says Saloni Sharma, MD, medical director of the Orthopaedic Integrative Health Center at Rothman Orthopaedics in Bryn Mawr, Pennsylvania. She suggests eating leafy greens, nuts, whole grains, and legumes to meet your magnesium needs.

L-theanine

Clients often ask about this amino acid, Moughal says. “It can promote relaxation and take the edge off anxiety, but it doesn’t act as a skeletal muscle relaxant or reduce muscle soreness directly. It’s more about calming the mind, which can be useful if tension is party stress-related.”

Should you take these supplements?

Supplements can “complement recovery, but they are not a substitute for evidence-based treatment,” Dr. Okoroha says. “They may help a little, but they are not miracle cures.”

Also, supplements aren’t regulated for safety and efficacy in the same way as medications, he adds. Dr. Swartzon says many social media or product claims are likely overstated, as well.

“Furthermore, they often lack any high-quality studies, make unrealistic claims, and can interact with other medications,” Dr. Swartzon says.

BEYOND SUPPLEMENTS, EXPERTS say there are several other “natural,” non-medication approaches for easing muscle aches and soreness, including:

Heat and ice therapy to reduce inflammation and relax tight muscles

Active recovery, such as walking or gentle stretching

Physical therapy to correct strength or mobility problems

Massage or foam rolling to improve tissue mobility

Adequate sleep, which is when most healing occurs

Hydration, since a lack of fluids and electrolytes may worsen cramping

Balanced nutrition, including protein and carbs, to support muscle function

Stress reduction, including breathing exercises, meditation, or yoga

Engaging in normal activities when you’re sore and avoiding resting too much

Warm-up and cool-down routines when exercising

“These methods often outperform supplements for day-to-day muscle soreness,” Okoroha says.

When to see a doctor

MUSCLE SORENESS AFTER mild-to-moderate exercises can be normal, Dr. Swartzon says. Often, these aches can be managed at home with one of the methods above, or with an over-the-counter topical cream for muscle soreness, Dr. Sharma says.

However, see your doctor if your pain lasts longer than a week or two or limits your normal activities, Dr. Okoroha says. Also, seek medical care if you have major swelling, bruising, weakness, or numbness, or if you heard a pop during an injury or can’t move the muscle or bear weight.

“These may be signs of a more serious injury,” Dr. Okoroha says.

And, if you find yourself taking over-the-counter pain medications, such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) like ibuprofen, too regularly, talk to your doctor about your pain, as these drugs may not be good for your gut or overall health, Moughal says.

Muscle soreness from training may be normal, but “persistent pain is not,” Dr. Okoroha says. “Natural remedies can offer small benefits, but they won’t fix an underlying injury. Focusing on proper recovery, mobility, technique, and listening to your body is far more effective than chasing quick fixes online.”
