THE LONGEVITY BOOM is real progress. Millions of men are finally taking their health seriously. They’re buying books, joining gyms, downloading apps, and building daily rituals designed to build strength and extend their lifespan. Still, too often, the focus stops at the physical.

Without another essential skill—emotion regulation—cold plunges, supplements, and performance metrics become scaffolding on shaky ground. Anxiety, frustration, and hopelessness don’t disappear because you track your sleep score. They have to be managed with skill.

You can hear this mind/body imbalance clearly in the spaces where men now go to learn how to live better lives. As my new book, Dealing with Feeling, has entered the public conversation, I’ve spent plenty of time on influential podcasts including Huberman Lab, The Rich Roll Podcast, Modern Wisdom with Chris Williamson, and School of Greatness with Lewis Howes. Each reaches millions of listeners, many of them men earnestly searching for a formula for a longer, stronger life. The same pillars reliably dominate: diet, exercise, sleep, hormones, supplements, performance. Yet one pillar rarely enters the conversation: how we deal with our emotions.

For men, that neglect is where the trouble starts. We’re raised to equate toughness with silence and self-reliance. Yet the research is unequivocal: unmanaged emotion undermines both length and quality of life. It shows that chronic anger is a stronger predictor of heart disease than many traditional risk factors. That loneliness carries the same mortality risk as smoking or obesity. And that anxiety and depression accelerate the decline of nearly every bodily system.

Meanwhile, many men invest in red light therapy and wearable trackers yet avoid the one skill that multiplies the value of every other health strategy.

Relationships as a Performance Asset

HARVARD'S 80-YEAR STUDY of adult development produced a headline finding: The single strongest predictor of health and lifespan is the quality of close relationships. Not income. Not cholesterol levels. Relationships.

Here the gender gap is striking. Men are more likely than women to report having few or no close friends, and more likely to withdraw under stress. The costs aren’t just personal. Emotional isolation erodes performance at home, at work, and in leadership roles.

The science of emotional contagion explains why. Emotions spread across teams and families. A spouse’s calm touch can reduce pain responses in the brain. A leader’s anxiety can ripple through a company’s workforce, undermining confidence and judgment. Conversely, leaders who regulate well set climates of steadiness that keep organizations resilient in turbulence.

In business, trust and clarity are the ultimate currencies. Unregulated emotion corrodes both. The executive who reacts in anger, panic, or shuts down doesn’t just shorten his own lifespan, he destabilizes his company. We’ve all seen it: a CEO’s mood, positive or negative, cascades down through the ranks. Companies live or die on culture, and culture is built on emotion.

The Skill Set Men Were Never Taught

FOR GENERATIONS, BOYS have been told to “man up,” “toughen up,” and “don’t cry.” Niobe Way, a highly respected developmental psychologist at NYU who has spent nearly 40 years studying boys’ and young men’s emotional lives and friendships, shows the cost of those norms. Her decades of research show that boys start with emotionally intimate friendships and often lose them as adolescence progresses, pushed by norms that equate vulnerability with weakness. By adulthood, many men are left without the emotional vocabulary or the trusted relationships that protect health and longevity. This disconnection has real consequences: in the U.S., men are about four times more likely to die by suicide than women, and they account for roughly three-quarters of deaths from suicide and drug overdoses. Suppression may look strong, but it corrodes from within—raising stress hormones, shortening tempers, and isolating men from the allies who could support them.

Here’s where definition matters. Many men assume “emotion regulation” means controlling or hiding emotions. But it’s the opposite. Emotion regulation is the ability to manage emotions intentionally and constructively to achieve goals. It’s what determines whether we can stick to the habits that help us thrive. When stress spikes, will we grab a drink, lash out at a colleague, or skip the gym? Or will we pause, breathe, and make choices that align with our goals and values?

The encouraging news: these regulation skills can be trained and strengthened at any stage of life. Many men already track contributors to what I call our “emotion regulation budget.” They track their workouts, diets, and sleep—the daily deposits and withdrawals that shape our capacity to respond wisely under pressure. But emotion regulation practices go beyond the physical, and these are the easiest ones to start learning:

Widen your mindset. All emotions matter. None are “bad.” Sadness signals loss. Anger signals injustice. Giving yourself permission to feel is the starting point. Rejecting emotions as weakness wastes valuable information; regulating them puts that data to work.

Label emotions with precision. Most men collapse a spectrum of feelings into “stressed” or “angry.” But there’s a world of difference between being irritated, overwhelmed, or betrayed. Knowing what you’re actually feeling gives you direction—so you can ask for the right support or take the best action instead of reacting on autopilot. It’s not always easy (that’s what inspired me to co-create an app called How We Feel that helps you pinpoint what you’re actually experiencing).

Quiet the mind and body before reacting. Think of it like an athletic reset. Before lifting a heavy weight, athletes breathe, pause, and center themselves. The same applies in conflict with a spouse, a colleague, or a teenager.

Reframe counterproductive thoughts. Men often run destructive internal scripts: “I’m failing.” “I have to do this alone.” Reframing doesn’t mean denial—it means replacing those scripts with more useful ones: “This setback doesn’t define me.” “I can ask for support and still be strong.”

Express anger or fear constructively. Anger, when unregulated, burns bridges. Expressed wisely, it clarifies values and earns respect. Fear, shared openly, can deepen bonds with a partner or a team.

Build emotional allies. Since the quality of our relationships is so connected to our health, happiness, and lifespan, a first step in building them is to reach out to others, recognizing that allies don’t make us dependent; they expand our capacity to lead, endure, and live well.

Redefining Strength and Success

FOR TOO LONG, men have measured themselves by material markers—net worth, muscle mass, job titles. But if we want lives that are not only long but meaningful, emotion regulation must become part of our identity and a new standard of success.

Mastering emotions doesn’t weaken manhood—it strengthens it. It makes you a steadier leader, a present partner, a stronger father, a loyal friend. It sharpens judgment, fortifies teams, and builds trust. Emotion regulation doesn’t suppress emotion; it turns it into fuel for performance and connection. If the goal is not just more years but better years, toughness must be redefined. True leverage in business, family, and life comes from one skill: emotion regulation. And the good news is that it’s 100 percent learnable at any age.
