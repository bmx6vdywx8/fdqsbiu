What tests are used to diagnose narcolepsy?

Narcolepsy is typically diagnosed using two key sleep studies performed back-to-back. What triggers a cataplexy attack and how can it be managed?

Cataplexy is a sudden, temporary loss of muscle tone triggered by strong emotions such as laughter, surprise, anger, or excitement. What type of tick transmits Lyme disease?

Lyme disease is transmitted by the black-legged tick, commonly known as the deer tick (*Ixodes scapularis*). Found primarily in the northeastern, mid-Atlantic, and upper midwestern United States, these ticks carry the bacterium *Borrelia burgdorferi*, which causes Lyme disease when transmitted through a bite. In western states, the closely related western black-legged tick (*Ixodes pacificus*) is the primary carrier. If you've been bitten by a tick or are experiencing symptoms like a bullseye rash, fatigue, fever, or joint pain, don't wait to find answers. Early identification is critical, as untreated Lyme disease can lead to serious long-term complications affecting the joints, heart, and nervous system. What's the difference between NSAIDs and steroids?

NSAIDs vs. Steroids: What's the Difference? NSAIDs (nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) and steroids both reduce inflammation and pain, but they work through different mechanisms and carry different risks. - **NSAIDs** (like ibuprofen, naproxen, and aspirin) block enzymes called COX-1 and COX-2 to reduce inflammation. Common side effects include stomach irritation, ulcers, and increased risk of heart or kidney issues with long-term use. - **Steroids** (like prednisone and cortisone) mimic hormones your body naturally produces to suppress the immune system and inflammation more broadly. Side effects can include weight gain, mood changes, high blood sugar, bone loss, and increased infection risk. **Key difference:** NSAIDs are typically used for mild to moderate pain and inflammation, while steroids are prescribed for more severe or chronic inflammatory conditions. When are Lyme disease ticks most active?

Lyme disease ticks—primarily blacklegged (deer) ticks—are most active during the warmer months, with peak activity in spring and summer. Nymphs, the tiniest and hardest-to-spot stage, are especially active from May through July, making this the highest-risk window for human bites and Lyme transmission. Adult ticks remain active into fall and can even bite on mild winter days above freezing. If you've recently been outdoors, spotted a tick, or noticed symptoms like a rash, fever, fatigue, or joint pain, don't wait to find out what's going on.
