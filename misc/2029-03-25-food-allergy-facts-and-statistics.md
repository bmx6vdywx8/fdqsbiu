What is a food allergy?

A food allergy is a medical condition in which certain foods trigger an individual’s adverse and reproducible allergic response. This allergic reaction occurs when the immune system attacks food proteins that are generally harmless in other individuals.1

Anaphylaxis is an acute, potentially deadly allergic reaction that causes the body to go into shock. Anaphylaxis caused by food allergies can be fatal if not immediately treated by epinephrine injection.

Allergic reactions to foods can include:2

Face, tongue, or lip swelling

Vomiting or diarrhea

Abdominal cramps

Hives

Flushed skin or rash

Tingling or itchy sensation in the mouth

Difficulty breathing

Coughing or wheezing

Dizziness or lightheadedness

Swelling of the throat and vocal cords

How many people have food allergies?

A total of about 32 million people in the U.S. have food allergies, including:4 6

26 million adults (10.8%)

5.6 million children (7.6%)

A report from 2018 showed that 4.8 million children under age 18 had food allergies over the past year, with nearly equal rates among white, Black, and Hispanic children.3 7

Among food-allergic adults, 45.3% are allergic to multiple foods, and 51.1% experienced a severe allergic reaction in the past year. Among children with food allergies, approximately 40% are allergic to more than one food.6

Are food allergies becoming more common?

The prevalence of childhood food allergies has steadily increased at a rate of 2.1% per decade among Blacks, 1.2% among Hispanics, and 1% among whites.8

According to the Centers for Disease Control & Prevention, the prevalence of food allergies among children rose from 3.4% in 1997 to 5.1% in 2011.9 Peanut and tree nut allergies among U.S. children more than tripled between 1997 and 2008.10

What are the economic impacts of food allergy reactions?

Each year, 200,000 Americans require emergency medical care for allergic reactions to food, with medical procedures to treat anaphylaxis increasing by nearly 380% between 2007 and 2016.11 12 13 Childhood hospitalizations for food allergy reactions tripled between the late 1990s and the mid-2000s.11 14

The overall economic cost of food allergies to children in the U.S. was estimated to be $24.8 billion annually in 2013, with direct medical costs accounting for $4.3 billion yearly.13

The importance of immediate treatment

When an allergy sufferer enters anaphylaxis (severe allergic reaction), they must immediately receive an epinephrine injection (adrenaline) to prevent a fatality. More than one dose may be necessary.15

Self-injectable epinephrine devices are available by prescription for food allergy sufferers and can potentially save lives.

Food allergy impacts on quality of life

Individuals with food allergies can endure significant life activity limitations and may qualify for protection under the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 (ADA) and Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973.11

Costs borne by the family due to food allergies totaled $20.5 billion in 2013 and included:5

Lost labor productivity

Out-of-pocket costs

Opportunity costs

Approximately one in three children with food allergies report being bullied due to their condition.16 Children with food allergies also often experience social isolation, with 15% of families avoiding restaurants and 10% homeschooling their children for fear of allergen exposure.17

Who is at the greatest risk for food allergies?

The following considerations demonstrate who is at the most significant risk for developing food allergies.

Race

Food allergies have increased among U.S. children during the past two decades, with the most significant increase seen among Black children.8 Today, Black children are at a greater risk of developing food allergies than non-Hispanic white children.18

Other health conditions

Patients with food allergies are two to four times more likely to have other conditions like atopic dermatitis (eczema), rhinitis, or asthma than those without food allergies. Food allergies are also associated with eosinophilic esophagitis, a chronic disorder of the digestive system.19

Location

Children in rural communities are less likely to develop food allergies than those in urban locations. Nearly 10% of children in urban areas develop food allergies, compared to about 6% in rural environments.20 Peanut and shellfish allergies, in particular, have double the prevalence or more in urban areas.21

Financial background

Children in households earning less than $50,000 per year are more likely to go undiagnosed with food allergies than those in households earning over $50,000.22

Age

Most food allergies develop in childhood, but at least 15% of patients are diagnosed later in life.23 Over 25% of adults with food allergies report a diagnosis during adulthood.4

Anaphylaxis

Teenagers and young adults are the most at risk for fatal food-induced anaphylaxis. Anaphylaxis is also more common among food allergy sufferers with asthma.24 25 26

Can you outgrow food allergies?

Allergies to milk, eggs, wheat, and soy often resolve in childhood, while allergies to tree nuts, peanuts, and shellfish are typically lifelong.27

Is there a cure?

While therapies are under study, there is no cure for food allergies.28 This condition must be managed by avoidance of problem foods and by recognizing symptoms.

Those who have allergies, their caretakers, and school supervisors should have access to epinephrine for immediate treatment in case of severe allergic reactions.
