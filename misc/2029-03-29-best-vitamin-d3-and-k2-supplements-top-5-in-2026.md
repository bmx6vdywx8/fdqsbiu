What is vitamin D3?

Vitamin D, in general, is an essential fat-soluble vitamin that can be produced from within the body (endogenously) and obtained from outside of the body (exogenously). Endogenous vitamin D is produced by exposure to sunlight, which converts a compound called 7-dehydrocholesterol into D3, whereas exogenous vitamin D comes from food or supplements.7

Vitamin D3 is one of the main forms in which vitamin D occurs. Taking a vitamin D3 supplement, you could potentially improve bone health, cognitive health, muscle strength, cancer risk, and mood disorders.21 22

The other major form of vitamin D is D2. As supplements, vitamins D2 and D3 differ in their:7

Production: D2 is plant-based, whereas D3 normally derives from sheep wool. There are exceptions, like Sports Research’s Vegadelight D3 derived from lichen.

Effect on blood serum levels: D3 raises blood serum levels more significantly and maintains them longer.

How much vitamin D do you need?

For most adult cohorts, the Recommended Daily Allowance (RDA) of exogenous vitamin D is 15mcg, or 600 International Units (IU), while the Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL) is 100mcg, or 4,000IU. In other words, people should generally aim to get at least 15mcg of vitamin D per day, but no more than 100mcg.7

Insider Tip: RDA and UL are two initialisms you’ll often encounter in discussions about nutrients. RDA refers to the average nutrient intake considered to be sufficient for meeting most healthy people’s needs, while UL is the maximum intake you can have in a day before you’re likely to experience adverse effects.

The RDA, however, doesn’t always account for populations that need larger doses. Specifically, most people with a vitamin D deficiency (i.e., serum levels below 20ng/mL) would be better off with a daily dose between 20 and 25mcg (800 and 1,000IU), and those with levels below 12ng/mL may need to start with stronger supplementation — 125mcg (5,000IU) to 150mcg (6,000IU) per day — to reach a healthy range.10 Once serum levels reach a normal range (above 30ng/mL), one can drop their daily intake to the RDA value.1

For the sub-12ng/mL cohort, an alternative supplementation method is to take an even higher dose — 625mcg (25,000IU) to 1,250mcg (50,000IU) — just once per week.1 11

Of course, you should speak with a medical professional before embarking on a vitamin D regimen, whether it’s low-dose or high-dose.

Causes and symptoms of vitamin D deficiency

Vitamin D deficiency often results from lifestyle factors, such as insufficient sunlight exposure and inadequate intake of vitamin D–rich foods (fish, yogurt, certain mushrooms).12 That being said, an individual’s vitamin D deficiency may also stem from factors outside of their control, such as:13

Darker skin pigmentation

Melanin can block the solar radiation needed to synthesize the vitamin endogenously.14

Kidney or liver failure

Both organs are responsible for converting stored vitamin D into the active form that the body can use.15

Medications

Certain medications can cause the liver to break down the body’s stores of vitamin D (e.g., blood pressure meds, seizure meds, estrogen blockers, antibiotics, antiretrovirals). Others (laxatives, lipase inhibitors, and cholesterol meds) may reduce the body’s ability to absorb the vitamin.16

Cancer

Multiple myeloma, breast cancer, prostate cancer, and colorectal cancer are associated with vitamin D deficiency.17

Other health conditions

Inflammatory bowel disease, celiac disease, and exocrine pancreatic insufficiency can make it difficult for the body to absorb enough vitamin D.

Gastrointestinal surgery

Bariatric surgery and small bowel resection may also affect the body’s absorption of vitamin D.

Genetic predisposition

Gene variations related to cholesterol synthesis, hydroxylation, and vitamin D transport may elevate a person’s risk for vitamin D deficiency.18

If you have a vitamin D deficiency, you may experience any or all of the following symptoms:19 13

Muscle and/or bone pain

Muscle weakness, especially in the upper arms or thighs

Muscle spasms, twitches, tremors, or cramps

Tingling in the hands or feet

An increased tendency for broken bones

Mood changes, including depression

Gait changes, such as waddling

Bowed legs (a classic symptom of rickets, which is usually due to prolonged vitamin D deficiency in childhood)20

The only surefire way to tell if you’re vitamin D-deficient is to have your vitamin D levels tested. You can order self-administered tests for home use, or ask your doctor to order a 25-hydroxy vitamin D test performed in a clinical setting.

What is vitamin K2?

Vitamins K and D are similar in that they’re both fat-soluble, capable of being produced endogenously, and obtainable through exogenous means. Both are also important for bone health, and vitamin K is especially critical for proper blood clotting.33

K2 specifically encompasses multiple isoprenyl side chains called menaquinones (MK), namely MK-4 through MK-14. The other vitamin K is K1, also known as phylloquinone.8 Between K1 and K2, there are three main differences:

Dietary sources: K1 derives primarily from leafy green vegetables like broccoli and spinach, whereas menaquinones are synthesized by bacteria, including those in the human gut, and are also found in animal products and fermented foods.8 23

Absorption: The body absorbs K2 more slowly than it does K1. Therefore, K2 remains in the body longer and has more time to deliver health benefits.24

Health benefits: Both forms of vitamin K are important for blood clotting, bone health, and heart health, but K2 may be more effective for the latter two. Additionally, some observational studies suggest a potential correlation between K2 intake and the clearance of calcifications that contribute to coronary heart disease.24 25 26

How much vitamin K do you need?

Vitamin K doesn’t have an RDA or UL attached to it; rather, it has an Adequate Intake (AI) value. AI takes the place of RDA when there isn’t enough research to establish an RDA.

Among adults, the AI value for combined vitamin K1 and K2 differs between men and women:8

Men: 120mcg

Women (including pregnant and lactating): 90mcg

As far as dietary sources go, 1–2 servings of green vegetables should get you over the threshold for K1. For K2, however, you’ll want to incorporate foods such as:27

Fermented foods (sauerkraut, kefir, natto)

Chicken breast and/or chicken liver

Ground beef

Salami

Egg yolks

Certain cheeses (e.g., Gouda, blue cheese)

But use discretion. Some of these foods may pose health risks (e.g., cardiovascular risks with cheese and processed meats, as well as cancer risks with the latter).28 29 30 You can circumvent such risks by taking a K2-inclusive supplement.

Causes and symptoms of vitamin K deficiency

Vitamin K deficiency, observed in around 8–31% of otherwise healthy people, may arise in adults who:31

Don’t eat enough K-rich foods: Vitamin K primarily comes from dietary sources, so it makes sense that overall K deficiency stems from a K-starved diet.

Have certain gastrointestinal medical conditions: Diseases like celiac disease and inflammatory bowel disease can reduce the body’s ability to absorb fat. Because vitamin K is fat-soluble, such diseases consequently hinder K absorption.

Take certain medications: Antibiotics can kill off the gut bacteria that synthesize vitamin K, and blood thinners can disrupt K metabolism.

Deficiency can also result from conditions during gestation or infancy. For example, exposure to maternal medications in the uterus can cause a baby to be born with a congenital deficiency, as can insufficient exposure to vitamin K during fetal development.32

Because vitamin K plays an integral role in blood clotting, the main symptom of K deficiency is excessive bleeding. Other symptoms may include:32

Petechiae (visible pinpoint-sized spots of bleeding under the skin)

Bruising, often on the head

Jaundiced or pale skin

Vomiting

Sleepiness

Seizures

Dark, sticky, and/or bloody stools

As with vitamin D, a K deficiency is diagnosed through testing. If you’re experiencing the above symptoms or have another reason to suspect that you’re deficient, please consult a medical provider.

Why should you take D3 and K2 together?

Vitamins D3 and K2 are pleiotropic nutrients with synergistic effects:

Capable of producing more than one effect (pleiotropic)

Capable of working together to increase the effect of any one component (synergistic)

In other words, D3 and K2 both impart numerous benefits in the body, and when taken together, they enhance each other’s benefits. To quote the authors of a 2017 review in the International Journal of Endocrinology: “Current evidence supports the notion that joint supplementation of vitamins D and K might be more effective than the consumption of either alone for bone and cardiovascular health.”34

Here’s an analogy to help illustrate. Say that a person wants to lose weight. In addition to restricting their calorie intake, they incorporate cardiovascular exercise and weight training. The cardio burns calories, and the weight training improves the body’s ability to use its calories more effectively toward weight loss. So, think of vitamin D3 as cardio and K2 as weight training, or vice versa. Combining them is a double-whammy that can maximize outcomes.

There’s also research suggesting that coadministration can help mitigate the adverse effects associated with high vitamin D doses, making the combination not only more effective but also potentially safer.46

Who should not take a vitamin D3/K2 supplement?

Not all D3/K2 supplements are appropriate for everyone. Most healthy adults can do with around 15mcg (600IU) per day, whereas almost all the recommendations in this guide provide higher doses and may not be broadly suitable for daily supplementation.

There are also several populations for whom D3/K2 supplementation may not be appropriate at all, or who should at least exercise caution. They include but aren’t limited to:41

People with already high levels of vitamin D (preferably verified by a vitamin D test)

People with high levels of calcium, as vitamins D and K can increase calcium absorption

People with a malabsorption disorder

People who are pregnant, as the supplement may affect the developing fetus

People with a granulomatous disease, such as sarcoidosis, in which the body overproduces vitamin D71

People with primary hyperthyroidism, which is characterized by hypercalcemia, which is itself associated with vitamin D excess72

People with primary hyperaldosteronism, which may lead to primary hyperthyroidism and its attendant risks associated with vitamin D72

People on certain medications, such as antibiotics, warfarin, orlistat, and prescriptions for lowering cholesterol33

Vitamin D, warfarin, kidney disease, and other concerns

We want to give special attention to the last point, especially regarding warfarin. Warfarin is an anticoagulant, a blood thinner, whereas vitamin K promotes blood clotting. Their functions are at odds with each other, such that the former can inhibit absorption of the latter, which is why a patient’s K intake must be delicately balanced with their warfarin dosage.70

This need for balance can have critical effects on warfarin, as well. Indeed, as noted by Dr. Matt Segar of our Medical Advisory Board: “Even tiny doses of vitamin K can change warfarin efficacy.”

It’s likewise important even among people for whom supplementation is indicated. People with kidney disease, for example, will need highly specific doses and protocols to counter their severe D deficiency.45

You can see, then, how important it is to consult a medical provider about your suitability for supplementation.

Of final note, to anyone looking to take a D3/K2 supplement, we urge you to temper expectations about health outcomes. Despite all of D3’s potential benefits, it isn’t going to harden brittle bones or beef up your body overnight. Rather, you should regard it as a small part of a larger health ecosystem that includes healthy eating, exercise, and regular medical care.

How safe are vitamin D3/K2 supplements?

Vitamin D3/K2 supplements are generally safe for most people who aren’t in danger of oversupplementation.

But people who already get sufficient exogenous vitamin D, such as by taking another D-inclusive supplement, may be at risk for oversupplementation, leading to an overabundance of the vitamin in the body. In addition to symptoms like weakness, nervousness, high blood pressure, and a loss of appetite, this can cause serious health conditions such as:42

Hypercalcemia

Hypercalcemia is an overabundance of calcium in the blood. Vitamin D’s connection to hypercalcemia lies in the vitamin’s ability to increase calcium absorption. People with hypercalcemia may have symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, muscle weakness, cognitive and neuropathic difficulties (including depression), and dehydration.7

Hypercalciuria

Hypercalcemia can, in turn, lead to hypercalciuria, or excess calcium in the urine. The calcium there can then form crystals that bind together to produce kidney stones.7

Vitamin D toxicity, however, is considered to be rare. It’s not enough to take a large dose of a D3/K2 supplement and then, bam, you have toxicity.
