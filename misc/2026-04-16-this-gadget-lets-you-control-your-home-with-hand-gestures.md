# ​This Gadget Lets You Control Your Home With Hand Gestures

When I tap the corner of the table, the Sonos speaker comes on, playing some low-key funk. Running my hand along an imaginary “slider” in midair turns up the volume. Moving my hand back down makes the music softer again. The guy I’m meeting with reaches out and taps another invisible “switch” nearby. A table fan whirs into action.

[Hand Gestures](https://beautybiometrics.hbsnzj.com/)

I’m in a loft in Brooklyn’s DUMBO neighborhood, meeting with the husband-wife team behind Hayo, Jose Alonso Ybañez Zepeda and Gisele Belliot. These self-described “tech addicts” are about to launch a device that connects your smart home devices, merging them into a single virtual remote control.

That means no more opening one app to adjust your Nest camera and another app to dim your Phillips Hue light bulbs. In fact, liberating people from a gazillion apps is the point.

"Take my mother for example," says Belliot. "When she visits me at home she cannot use the smart TV, the Sonos, the cameras, the 'smart' coffee maker. She's like, 'How does it work?' I say, 'You just need the app.' She says, 'I don't want another app!'"

And, truly, if our homes are getting “smarter,” shouldn’t our guests be able to make a cup of coffee or turn on the Roku without figuring out a whole new interface each time? That was what Zepeda and Belliot were thinking in 2010, when they found themselves chatting with Dale Herigstad, the guy who designed the interactive sequences in the film Minority Report. Remember those scenes where Tom Cruise swipes around graphics in midair using gloves with glowing fingertips? Zepeda, a PhD fascinated by 3D sensing technology, thought, "I could actually make something like this."

How Hayo works: It's a cylindrical gadget that looks like a slightly curvier Amazon Echo. It contains 3D sensors that map the environment around the device—up to 6 meters away, so it's meant for a single room. Then, you tell it where you want to place invisible buttons, sliders and barrier fields in the space, and link each to a command for one of your connected devices via its app.

[Lets You Control](https://thymicshield.dns0663.com/)

For example, you can place an invisible "button" on the arm of the couch. Now instead of getting up to turn off the lights when you're ready to watch a movie, you just hover your hand on this button for a second and—poof—your smart lightbulbs go dark.

Maybe you want to make coffee in the morning without getting out of bed? If you have your coffeemaker plugged into a connected outlet (like one from Belkin or a similar brand), you can link that outlet to an invisible button on your bedside table. You tap the button. Hayo communicates with the outlet. Your coffee is brewing while you roll over and snooze.

"With the placement of these virtual switches, it's not like you have to call an electrician if you want to move one," says Zepeda. "If you don't like it, you can move it. Or maybe you need a second one—you can add it. It's your home, you know how you move through it, and you can customize your space."

Plus, there’s the benefit of corralling all your WiFi-enabled devices. Take Hayo’s “barrier field." You could program it so that as soon as you cross the threshold of your apartment, the lights come on, the thermostat adjusts, and your Nest security cameras swivel to the wall so that you aren’t paranoid they’re recording you.

After a successful Indiegogo campaign that ended in March, Belliot and Zepeda are preparing to ship the first Hayo devices this fall (pre-order at hayo.io). Each Hayo is $299, and so far they're able to connect to more than 270,000 smart appliances and services—plus, Hayo's API kit is available to developers so the list of compatible apps is always growing.

Sitting in the Hayo loft, adjusting the music with a wave of my hand, makes me feel like a hybrid of Steve Jobs and Harry Potter. My mind immediately goes to all the things I could do with it in my living room, and the shocked expressions I'd get if I skipped to the next song on my Sonos playlist by nonchalantly tapping a houseplant with my foot.

But, funny enough, it’s not just the sense of “magic” that makes it appealing. It’s the idea that I could operate all my wireless gadgets and apps in a physical way. After all, I spend enough of my day hunched over my iPhone. Can’t I at least put down the phone when I get home? Zepeda agrees. "When you're at home, you want to relax," he says. "You're listening to music and when you go to open the Sonos app on your phone you see... I have an email, I have a Facebook notification, I have a tweet. Suddenly you've done an hour of work instead of relaxing."

Relaxing sounds good. But first let me adjust the AC by putting my feet on the coffee table.
