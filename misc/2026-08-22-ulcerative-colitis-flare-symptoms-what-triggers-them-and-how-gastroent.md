What Is an Ulcerative Colitis Flare?

An ulcerative colitis (UC) flare is a period of worsening colon inflammation that causes abdominal pain, urgent bloody diarrhea, fatigue, and sometimes joint or skin symptoms.

Explanation

Ulcerative colitis is a chronic condition that causes long-term inflammation and ulcers (sores) in the lining of the large intestine (colon) and rectum. People with ulcerative colitis often experience periods of remission—when symptoms ease or disappear—and flares, when symptoms worsen. Understanding common ulcerative colitis symptoms, learning what can trigger a flare, and knowing how gastroenterologists manage them can help you stay informed and prepared.

Common Ulcerative Colitis Symptoms

During a flare, inflammation in the colon intensifies. Symptoms vary from mild to severe, but often include:

Abdominal cramping and pain

Frequent, urgent bowel movements

Bloody stools or visible blood on toilet tissue

Diarrhea, often with mucus

Fatigue and weakness

Loss of appetite and unintended weight loss

Fever (in moderate to severe flares)

Anemia (from chronic blood loss)

Although these are the hallmark signs, some people may also experience joint pain, skin rashes, or eye inflammation. Keep track of your personal pattern of symptoms and share it with your care team.

What Triggers Ulcerative Colitis Flares?

No single cause has been identified for ulcerative colitis flares. However, several factors can increase the likelihood of symptom worsening:

Medication Changes or Non-Adherence

Skipping or stopping prescribed medications can let inflammation resurge.

Always discuss dose changes or side effects with your gastroenterologist before making adjustments.

Infections

Gastrointestinal infections (viral, bacterial, or parasitic) can irritate the colon.

No universal "flare diet" exists, but certain foods (spicy, high-fiber, dairy, caffeine) may trigger symptoms in some people.

Smoking Cessation

Although smoking is unhealthy, some people with ulcerative colitis notice a mild protective effect from nicotine.

Quitting smoking may transiently increase flare risk.

Hormonal Changes

Some people report flares around pregnancy, menstruation, or menopause.

Hormonal fluctuations may affect the gut lining and immune response.

How Gastroenterologists Manage Ulcerative Colitis Flares

When a flare occurs, the goal is to reduce inflammation quickly, relieve symptoms, and restore remission. Your gastroenterologist will tailor treatment based on the severity of your flare and your overall health.

1. Medication Strategies

5-Aminosalicylic Acid (5-ASA) Agents• First-line for mild to moderate flares• Delivered orally, rectally (suppositories/enemas), or both

Corticosteroids• Oral or intravenous steroids for moderate to severe flares• Used short-term (usually ≤ 12 weeks) to induce remission

Immunomodulators• Azathioprine or 6-mercaptopurine to reduce immune system overactivity• Useful for steroid-dependent patients or maintaining remission

Biologic Therapies• Anti-TNF agents (infliximab, adalimumab), integrin receptor antagonists (vedolizumab), or interleukin inhibitors (ustekinumab)• Reserved for moderate to severe disease or inadequate response to other treatments

Janus Kinase (JAK) Inhibitors• Tofacitinib approved for moderate to severe ulcerative colitis• Oral medication that blocks specific inflammatory pathways

2. Supportive Treatments

Antidiarrheals (e.g., loperamide)• Short-term relief of diarrhea, used cautiously

Pain Management• Acetaminophen for mild pain• Avoid NSAIDs unless approved by your doctor

Nutritional Support• Dietary adjustments to manage dehydration, blood loss, and malnutrition• Sometimes temporary use of liquid nutrition or feeding tubes if severe

3. Monitoring and Follow-Up

Regular Colonoscopies• Assess mucosal healing and screen for dysplasia (precancerous changes)• Frequency depends on disease duration and severity

Lab Tests• Complete blood count (CBC) to check for anemia• Inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein, fecal calprotectin) to gauge activity

Symptom Tracking• Keep a diary of bowel habits, pain levels, diet, and stressors• Share records with your gastroenterologist at each visit

4. Lifestyle and Self-Care

Diet Modifications• Low-residue or low-fiber diet during flares to minimize irritation• Lactose-free or dairy-reduced if lactose intolerance is an issue

Hydration• Drink plenty of water to replace fluid losses• Oral rehydration solutions if diarrhea is severe

Stress Management• Mindfulness, yoga, deep-breathing exercises, or talk therapy

Regular Exercise• Low-impact activities (walking, swimming) to boost mood and overall health

Smoking Cessation Support• Counseling or nicotine replacement under medical guidance

Recognizing Warning Signs: When to Seek Immediate Help

Some symptoms may signal serious complications and require prompt medical attention:

Severe, persistent abdominal pain

High fever (> 101°F or 38.3°C)

Heavy rectal bleeding or passing large blood clots

Signs of dehydration (dizziness, rapid heartbeat, dark urine)

Inability to eat or drink for more than 24 hours

Key Takeaways

Ulcerative colitis flares involve increased inflammation in the colon, leading to abdominal pain, diarrhea, and bleeding.

Common triggers include medication lapses, infections, certain pain relievers, stress, and dietary factors.

Gastroenterologists manage flares with medications (5-ASA, steroids, immunomodulators, biologics), supportive care, and regular monitoring.

Lifestyle adjustments—diet, hydration, exercise, and stress reduction—play a vital role in symptom control.

Recognize warning signs of complications and seek immediate care if they occur.

Always remember: if you experience any potentially life-threatening or serious symptoms, speak to a doctor without delay. Your healthcare team is your best resource for managing ulcerative colitis symptoms and maintaining remission.
