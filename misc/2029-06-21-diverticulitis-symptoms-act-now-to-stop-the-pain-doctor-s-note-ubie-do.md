Diverticulitis Symptoms: Why Your Colon Is Inflamed & Medically Approved Next Steps

Diverticulitis symptoms can be uncomfortable, confusing, and sometimes serious. If you've been dealing with lower abdominal pain, fever, or sudden digestive changes, your colon may be inflamed due to a condition called diverticulitis.

The good news? Most cases can be treated effectively—especially when caught early. Below, you'll learn what diverticulitis is, the most common symptoms, why it happens, and what medically approved next steps you should take.

What Is Diverticulitis?

Diverticulitis happens when small pouches in the colon (called diverticula) become inflamed or infected.

These pouches form over time, especially after age 40. The presence of pouches alone is called diverticulosis, and many people have it without knowing. However, when one or more pouches become blocked and infected, that's when diverticulitis symptoms begin.

The inflammation most often affects the lower part of the large intestine (colon), particularly on the left side.

Common Diverticulitis Symptoms

Symptoms can range from mild to severe. They usually develop over hours to a few days.

Most Common Symptoms

Persistent lower left abdominal pain (most common symptom)

Fever

Chills

Nausea or vomiting

Constipation or diarrhea

Bloating

Loss of appetite

The abdominal pain often feels:

Constant (not crampy like gas pain)

Worse with movement or pressure

Focused in one specific area

Some people describe it as a deep, steady ache.

Less Common but Serious Symptoms

In more severe cases, diverticulitis can lead to complications. Seek medical care urgently if you experience:

Severe or worsening abdominal pain

High fever (over 101°F / 38.3°C)

Persistent vomiting

Why Does Diverticulitis Happen?

Doctors believe diverticulitis develops when one of the small pouches becomes blocked—often by stool or bacteria—leading to inflammation and infection.

Risk Factors Include:

Age over 40 (risk increases with age)

Low-fiber diet

Obesity

Smoking

Physical inactivity

Certain medications (like NSAIDs or steroids)

How Is Diverticulitis Diagnosed?

If your doctor suspects diverticulitis based on your symptoms, they may order:

CT scan of the abdomen (most accurate test)

Blood tests (to check for infection or inflammation)

Urine test (to rule out urinary causes)

Medically Approved Treatment Options

Treatment depends on how severe the diverticulitis symptoms are.

1. Mild (Uncomplicated) Diverticulitis

Many mild cases can be treated at home with:

Oral antibiotics (in some cases; not always required)

Clear liquid diet for a few days

Gradual return to low-fiber foods, then normal diet

Rest

Pain management (as recommended by a doctor)

Recent medical guidelines show that some mild cases may improve without antibiotics, under medical supervision.

Symptoms typically improve within a few days of treatment.

2. Moderate to Severe Diverticulitis

Hospital treatment may be needed if you have:

Severe pain

High fever

Inability to tolerate fluids

Other health conditions

Signs of complications

Hospital care may include:

IV antibiotics

IV fluids

Pain control

Imaging monitoring

In rare cases, surgery may be necessary if:

There is a perforation

Repeated attacks occur

An abscess does not respond to drainage

There is bowel obstruction

Most people do not require surgery.

Recovery: What to Expect

For uncomplicated diverticulitis:

Pain improves within 2–3 days

Full recovery usually occurs within 1–2 weeks

Fatigue may last longer

After recovery, your doctor may recommend:

Gradually increasing fiber intake

Regular exercise

Weight management

Smoking cessation (if applicable)

These steps may help reduce the risk of future episodes.

Can Diverticulitis Come Back?

Yes, it can. About 20–40% of people may experience another episode.

However:

Many people never have a second attack

Repeat episodes are often similar or milder

Serious complications are uncommon

Long-term management focuses on lifestyle adjustments rather than surgery in most cases.

When Should You See a Doctor?

Speak to a doctor promptly if you experience:

New or persistent lower abdominal pain

Fever with digestive symptoms

Symptoms lasting more than 24–48 hours

Pain that is worsening rather than improving

Seek emergency care immediately if you have:

Severe abdominal pain

Fainting

Heavy rectal bleeding

Vomiting that prevents fluids

A hard, rigid abdomen

It is always safer to have concerning abdominal pain evaluated.

While not all cases are preventable, you can reduce risk by:

Eating a fiber-rich diet (fruits, vegetables, whole grains)

Drinking plenty of water

Exercising regularly

Maintaining a healthy weight

Avoiding smoking

Following your doctor's recommendations after an episode

Fiber should be increased gradually to avoid bloating.

Key Takeaways About Diverticulitis Symptoms

The most common diverticulitis symptom is steady lower left abdominal pain.

Fever and digestive changes often accompany the pain.

Most cases are treatable and improve with prompt care.

Serious complications are uncommon but possible.

Early medical evaluation helps prevent worsening infection.

Lifestyle adjustments can lower recurrence risk.
