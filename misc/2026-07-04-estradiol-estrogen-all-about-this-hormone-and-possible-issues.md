# Estradiol (Estrogen) - All About This Hormone And Possible Issues

Estradiol (Estrogen) – All About This Hormone And Possible Issues

Estradiol, a highly strong estrogen, has a pivotal function in the reproductive system, maintaining bone health, and providing neuroprotection. It controls the development of female reproductive organs, the changes that occur throughout puberty, and the different phases of the menstrual cycle. Elevated estradiol levels can contribute to a range of health complications, such as cancer, whereas diminished levels can result in osteoporosis and impact mood and sexual function. Estradiol levels often differ between males and females, with treatment options encompassing systemic hormone therapy and low-dose vaginal preparations. Regularly monitoring estradiol levels is crucial for effectively managing associated health issues and maintaining optimal overall health.

What Is Estradiol?

Estradiol is the most potent estrogen (E2) in the human body.1 It is a hormone made by the body and present in the ovaries. Estradiol in males is produced by the Sertoli cells of the testes.

What Does Estradiol Do In Our Bodies?

In females, estradiol acts primarily as a growth hormone for the reproductive organs, including the cervical glands, the endometrium, the fallopian tubes, and the vagina. Estradiol also helps to grow the womb’s muscle layer (the myometrium). Additionally, estradiol maintains oocytes (eggs in the ovary) and triggers a series of events that lead to ovulation.

Estradiol drives the changes that happen around puberty. These changes are enhanced during the reproductive years and then become less pronounced after menopause as estradiol levels decline. Estradiol is required for the alteration of body shape, normal breast development, skin changes, and the fat distribution profile that is typical of females.

Estradiol is necessary for the preservation of the eggs inside a female’s ovaries.

During the menstrual cycle, follicles on the ovaries secrete estradiol, which triggers a surge in luteinizing hormone and, in turn, includes ovulation. This phase of the cycle is known as the follicular phase. After ovulation, during the luteal phase, estradiol and progesterone prepare the womb lining for implantation.

Estradiol levels rise during pregnancy because it is also produced by the placenta. Estradiol may also play a role in maintaining the pregnancy. Research is being done about the role of estrogen in initiating labor.

A healthy level of estradiol is also needed to support adequate bone growth and maintain the health of bones and joints. Estradiol also impacts the bone structure and osteopenia. Osteoporosis can occur if estradiol levels are low. Bone loss can be accelerated in women of post-menopausal age who may have a relative deficiency in estradiol.

Estradiol is also produced in the brain, and estrogens have been shown to have neuroprotective effects. Estrogens can affect vasculature and may help improve blood flow in the coronary arteries.

Estrogens are also involved in several non-cancerous conditions, including endometriosis, uterine bleeding, and uterine fibroids.

Normal Estradiol Levels In Men and Women

Normal estradiol levels in men are 10 to 50 picograms per milliliter (pg/mL). Normal levels for women will vary depending on age. For example, normal estradiol levels for premenopausal women are 30 to 400 pg/mL while normal levels for post-menopausal women are 0 to 30 pg/mL.

High Levels Of Estradiol

In women, high levels of estradiol are linked to acne, constipation, depression, and loss of sex drive. Extremely high levels of estradiol may lead to uterine and breast cancer. Women with high estradiol levels may also experience weight gain and cardiovascular disease. In men, it can lead to developing female characteristics and loss of sexual function or muscle tone.

Low Levels Of Estradiol

Estrogen levels begin to decline during perimenopause, the first stage of menopause. Some medical conditions can also cause decreases in estrogen. In females, conditions that affect the ovaries can decrease both estrogen and estradiol levels. In males, conditions that cause low testosterone may also cause low estrogen.

Examples of health conditions or circumstances that may cause low estrogen include:

Breastfeeding

Chemotherapy

Congenital conditions such as Turner syndrome

Low body weight

Low-functioning pituitary gland

PCOS

Primary ovarian insufficiency

Recently giving birth

Thyroid disorders

What To Expect During An Estradiol Test?

An estradiol test requires a blood sample. A medical professional will take the blood sample from your arm or hand.

How To Prepare For An Estradiol Test?

You do not need to fast before an estradiol test. You need to inform your doctor if you are taking any medication, such as birth control or hormone therapy medication before you have the test done. Certain medications could affect the test results.

How Long Does It Take To Get The Results?

Your doctor will likely take a few days to get the test results.

Systemic hormone therapy is available in several forms, including:

Systemic estrogen therapy typically contains a higher dose of estrogen that your body can be absorbed. This treatment may help treat common symptoms of menopause. Prices for systemic hormone therapy can vary from $10 a month for a 90-day supply of generic estrogen pills to more than $500 for a 90-day vaginal ring.

Low-Dose Vaginal Products

Low-dose preparations of estrogen are available in cream, tablet, or ring form. Because these products minimize the amount of estrogen absorbed by the body, they are usually only used to treat the vaginal and urinary symptoms of menopause. Prices for low-dose vaginal products can range from $34 to $677.
